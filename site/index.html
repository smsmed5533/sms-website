    The Stigma Surrounding STDs

    Stigma keeps people from getting tested and treated. Here's where it comes from and how to push back on it.

    SMS Medical

    Sexually transmitted diseases (STDs) are infections that can be passed through sexual contact with an infected person. Despite being common, there is a significant stigma surrounding STDs. This stigma can make it difficult for people to seek medical care or tell their partners about a diagnosis, and it can lead to feelings of shame and guilt.

    One reason for the stigma is the misconception that only promiscuous people get these infections. In reality, anyone who is sexually active can contract an STD. Many STDs can be passed even when people practice safer sex, such as using condoms.

    Another source of stigma is the way people with STDs are portrayed in the media and popular culture. These portrayals often suggest that people with STDs are unclean, immoral, or deserving of shame. In reality, STDs are a widespread health problem that affects people of all ages, races, genders, and sexual orientations.

    Why stigma matters

    The stigma surrounding STDs can have serious consequences. It can delay testing and treatment, and it can contribute to anxiety and depression. It can also make it harder for people to tell their sexual partners about a diagnosis, which can increase how often infections are passed on.

    How we can reduce it

    Fighting stigma starts with education about the realities of these infections, including dispelling myths like the idea that only promiscuous people get STDs. Healthcare providers also play a vital role by offering non-judgmental, confidential care. Together, these steps help reduce stigma and build a more inclusive and supportive community.

    The Relationship Between STDs and Mental Health

    A diagnosis can bring anxiety, guilt, and isolation. Taking care of your mental health is part of taking care of your health.

    SMS Medical

    Sexually transmitted diseases (STDs) can have a significant impact on a person's mental health. A diagnosis can bring a wide range of emotional and psychological reactions, including anxiety, depression, guilt, and shame.

    One reason is the social stigma surrounding STDs, which can make people feel isolated, ashamed, and judged. They may worry about how others will see them, or feel they have to keep their diagnosis a secret. This can lead to loneliness and depression, which can get worse over time if left unaddressed.

    How some infections affect mental health

    Certain STDs can affect mental health more directly. Some research suggests that people with herpes simplex virus (HSV) may be more likely to experience symptoms of anxiety and depression. People with human papillomavirus (HPV) may feel anxiety and stress about the increased risk of cervical cancer. A diagnosis can also affect sexual relationships, which can add to anxiety and depression.

    Getting support

    It's important for people with STDs to prioritize their mental health and to seek support when they need it. Talking with a healthcare provider or a mental health professional is a good first step. Support groups and online communities can also offer a safe, non-judgmental space to connect with others going through similar experiences.

    The relationship between STDs and mental health is complex. Seeking support and caring for your mental health is an important part of your overall health and wellness.

    The Often Overlooked STD: Trichomoniasis

    Trich is common, often has no symptoms, and is easy to treat once it's found. Here's what to know.

    SMS Medical

    Trichomoniasis, also known as "trich," is a common sexually transmitted infection (STI) caused by a parasite called Trichomonas vaginalis. With an estimated 156 million cases each year, it is also one of the most treatable STIs worldwide.

    Trichomoniasis is mainly spread through sexual contact (vaginal, oral, or anal sex) with an infected partner. The parasite is most often found in the vagina, but it can also infect the urethra in men. Although trichomoniasis is not considered a serious infection, it can cause discomfort and complications if left untreated.

    Symptoms of trichomoniasis

    Most people with trichomoniasis have no symptoms. When symptoms do appear, they usually show up 5 to 28 days after infection and may include:

    • A thin, foamy, greenish-yellow or gray discharge with an unpleasant odor
    • Pain, itching, or burning during urination or sex
    • Genital inflammation or swelling
    • Discomfort during sex
    • Stomachache

    Diagnosis

    Trichomoniasis is diagnosed with laboratory tests that detect the parasite. Your healthcare provider may collect a swab from the vagina or urethra. For men, a urine test may be used instead.

    Treatment

    Trichomoniasis is treated with a prescription antibiotic, usually metronidazole, given either as a single dose or as a 7-day course depending on the patient. To prevent reinfection, both partners should start treatment at the same time and avoid sex or use condoms until treatment is complete.

    Prevention

    The best way to prevent trichomoniasis is to practice safer sex, including using condoms and limiting the number of sexual partners. Women can also lower their risk by avoiding douching, which can disturb the natural balance of bacteria in the vagina.

    Why get tested?

    Trichomoniasis is one of the most frequently reported STIs in Illinois. The Illinois Department of Public Health reported more than 12,000 cases in the state in 2020, a 20% increase from 2019.

    If you think you may have trichomoniasis, you and your partner should both get tested so you can receive the right treatment and avoid complications.

    Questions

    What is DNA paternity testing?

    A DNA paternity test compares genetic markers from the alleged father, the child, and ideally the mother to determine whether the alleged father is the biological father.

    How accurate is it?

    DNA paternity testing is highly accurate and can determine paternity with a probability of 99.9% or higher. We test more than 40 DNA markers for accurate results.

    How is the sample collected?

    With a buccal swab: a soft swab rubbed on the inside of the cheek. It's quick and painless, and the samples are then analyzed by an accredited DNA laboratory.

    What's the difference between legal and peace-of-mind tests?

    A legal test uses chain-of-custody collection and ID verification for everyone tested, so results can be used for court, custody, child support, or immigration matters. A peace-of-mind test gives the same answer for your own knowledge but can't be used legally.

      Billing & insurance.

      How we bill for lab testing and imaging, what to bring, and who to call with questions.

      Insurance we accept

      We bill Medicare, Medicaid, and most commercial insurance plans directly. Bring your insurance card and a photo ID to your visit, and give your provider’s office your current insurance so it can go on your lab order. Not sure your plan is covered? Call us at (708) 424-9405 before your visit.

      After your test

      • We send the claim to your insurance plan. You don’t need to file it yourself.
      • Your plan sends you an Explanation of Benefits (EOB). It isn’t a bill.
      • If you owe a deductible, copay, coinsurance, or an amount your plan didn’t cover, you’ll receive a statement from us with payment instructions.
      • Questions about a statement? Call us at (708) 424-9405 and have the statement in front of you.

      Medicare and the ABN

      Medicare doesn’t cover every test in every situation. If we believe Medicare may not pay for a test, you may be asked to sign an Advance Beneficiary Notice (ABN) before testing. The ABN lets you decide whether to have the test and accept responsibility for payment if Medicare denies it.

      Long-term care residents

      For residents in a Medicare Part A covered stay, lab and imaging services are generally billed to the facility under Medicare’s consolidated billing rules. Otherwise, we bill Medicare Part B, Medicaid, or the resident’s insurance. Families with questions can call us at (708) 424-9405.

      Paying yourself

      Self-pay prices for STD testing and DNA paternity testing are listed on those pages and are confirmed when you check in. Prefer not to use insurance for other tests? Call us for a price first.

      Practices and facilities

      Client-billed services are invoiced to your practice or facility as described in your services agreement. For account billing questions, call client services at (708) 424-9405.

      Your right to a Good Faith Estimate

      You have the right to receive a “Good Faith Estimate” explaining how much your medical care will cost.

      Under the law, health care providers need to give patients who don’t have insurance or who are not using insurance an estimate of the bill for medical items and services.

      • You have the right to receive a Good Faith Estimate for the total expected cost of any non-emergency items or services. This includes related costs like medical tests, prescription drugs, equipment, and hospital fees.
      • Make sure your health care provider gives you a Good Faith Estimate in writing at least 1 business day before your medical service or item. You can also ask your health care provider, and any other provider you choose, for a Good Faith Estimate before you schedule an item or service.
      • If you receive a bill that is at least $400 more than your Good Faith Estimate, you can dispute the bill.
      • Make sure to save a copy or picture of your Good Faith Estimate.

      For questions or more information about your right to a Good Faith Estimate, visit www.cms.gov/nosurprises or call 1-800-985-3059.

      Standard precautionPrescribe as usual. No significant drug-gene interaction found.
      Use with cautionModerate interaction. Dose adjustment or added monitoring may be warranted.

      Where it changes prescribing

      Behavioral health
      SSRIs, SNRIs, tricyclics, antipsychotics
      Cardiology

      Pain
      Codeine, tramadol, oxycodone, NSAIDs
      GI

      Multiple medications
      Patients on several interacting medications

      Simple cheek-swab collection

      1. No oral hygiene products or nasal gels for 30 minutes before.
      2. Two swabs, one per cheek, 10 seconds each.
      3. Each swab in its own dry tube, labeled with two identifiers.
      4. Store dry at room temperature. Send with the signed consent and a current medication list.

      Before you order

      Germline PGx is generally a once-in-a-lifetime test, and payers look for clear medical necessity. Check these first:

      • A specific drug decision. You’re prescribing, or considering, a medication with an actionable gene-drug interaction.
      • The treating prescriber orders it. Not a standing or facility-wide order.
      • Documented medical necessity. The chart shows the diagnosis, the drug(s) under consideration, and how results will guide treatment.
      • No prior PGx test. Ask the patient and check records first, from any lab.
      • Consent and a current medication list go with the specimen.

      Coverage varies by payer, and not every gene has established prescribing guidance. Call us with coverage questions before ordering.
